# What and how to record due diligence, evidence

Chapter 1 establishes that [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence is part of the process the manufacturer
carries out, to test whether the finished product meets the essential cybersecurity
requirements of Annex I. A process leaves no trace unless the manufacturer records it. This
chapter covers what that record contains and who has to be able to read it afterwards.

The CRA does not prescribe a form for the due diligence record and does not place it in a separate
obligation. It seems instead to arrive through the technical documentation. [Article
31][article-31] and Annex VII require the manufacturer to document the assessments carried out,
and [Article 13(13)][article-13] requires manufacturers to "keep the technical documentation and
the EU declaration of conformity at the disposal of the market surveillance authorities for at
least 10 years after the product with digital elements has been placed on the market or for the
support period, whichever is longer". Whoever reads the record was not present when the
manufacturer made the check, and on that retention floor may be reading it a decade afterwards.

## Levels of due diligence

[Recital 34][recital-34] states that the appropriate level of due diligence "depends on the nature
and the level of cybersecurity risk associated with a given component". A component that parses
untrusted input in a privileged context and a component that formats log timestamps do not warrant
the same effort.

The level sets the depth of the checks and the evidence retained for each. It does not govern
whether a record exists at all. A component assessed as low risk still produces a record, and that
record carries the reason the manufacturer set the level where it did. The level is itself a
finding, so its justification belongs alongside the checks it governed. A record that simply omits
the low-risk components is indistinguishable from the record of an assessment that missed them.

## What has to be checked

Chapter 1 sets out three elements, Visibility, Vigilance and Maintenance. Each produces
observations the manufacturer can write down, and the methods listed in [Recital 34][recital-34]
apply across them.

Under Visibility the manufacturer records the component's identity, its exact version and its
declared dependencies. Annex I,
Part II, point (1) requires the manufacturer in any case to "identify and document vulnerabilities
and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a software
bill of materials in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the
top-level dependencies of the products".

Vigilance is a position at a moment, so it is recorded as one: which databases were consulted on
what date, and what each of them returned, a nil return included. [Recital 34][recital-34]
names the European vulnerability database established pursuant to Article 12(2) of Directive (EU)
2022/2555, and other publicly accessible vulnerability databases.

Maintenance concerns the project's capacity to respond. [Recital 34][recital-34] points to the
security updates history, and a disclosure policy shows the same thing by its presence and its
content. Where an open-source software steward supports the project, [Article 24(1)][article-24]
requires that steward to "put in place and document in a verifiable manner a cybersecurity
policy".

The remaining method in [Recital 34][recital-34], carrying out additional security tests, differs
from the other three in what it leaves behind. The first three record something a third party can
look up and find again. A test records a result that only the party running it observed. A test
result is therefore worth recording with its inputs and the time it ran, so that someone else can
repeat it against the same component version.

The concrete properties to check are those in [Properties to check for Article 13(5) due
diligence](01.md#properties-to-check-for-article-135-due-diligence) in chapter 1: project identity
and authenticity, development practices, governance, and vulnerability handling. The record leads
with the manufacturer's final result for the component, and under it keeps the outcome of each
property checked, at the depth set out in [How much of the record to keep](#how-much-of-the-record-to-keep).

## What a record has to contain

Due diligence checks how a component is developed and maintained, not the integration itself,
and what it finds can change after the check. The record therefore states when each check was
made. It identifies the component by its Package URL
([PURL](https://github.com/package-url/purl-spec)) with the exact version, rather than defining
its own way to identify a package. A record naming a project and a version range such as `2.x`
covers many releases, so it cannot be re-checked against the one that shipped.

One component can be integrated into many products, so the record holds no product information.
Each product keeps a list of the records it relies on, and one record serves every product that
integrates that component version.

The CRA does not enumerate fields for this record, so the table that follows is a proposal rather
than a reading of the text. As best we can tell, a record that a later reader can re-perform has
to state at least the following.

| What the record states | Why it is in the record |
| --- | --- |
| The component, identified by its PURL with the exact version | A PURL is an existing standard for identifying a package. A version range covers many releases, while a PURL with a version names one |
| What was checked, and against which source | A later reader consults the same source and sees whether the answer has moved since the check |
| Each maintainer-controlled metadata field, such as the security contact and the source repository, as the manufacturer read it, with where and when | These values change upstream without a new release. The record shows what the manufacturer relied on at the time of the check, so the manufacturer can answer a market surveillance authority accurately after a value has changed. [Article 64(4)][article-64] sets fines for "the supply of incorrect, incomplete or misleading information to notified bodies and market surveillance authorities in reply to a request" |
| The final result for the component, and the outcome of each property checked, including outcomes that found nothing | The final result is what the manufacturer decided, and the property outcomes are what the decision rested on. A nil outcome is a check that ran, and an omission is not distinguishable from one that never ran |
| What was done about any finding, which under [Recital 34][recital-34] includes informing the person or entity manufacturing or maintaining the component | The recital names the action, so the record of the action completes the check |
| Who or what performed the check, the date and time it was performed, and the version of any tool used | What a check finds describes the project at that moment and can change afterwards. Two versions of a scanner give two answers about the same component |

The rows on what was checked and on the outcome of each property separate a record of a check
from a record of a conclusion. A conclusion
reports what the manufacturer decided. A check with its inputs supports repeating it, and a market surveillance
authority is in a position to ask what repeating it would yield.

## How much of the record to keep

A record holding only the final result for each component would be the smallest, but a final
result on its own cannot be re-checked. [Article 31(1)][article-31] requires the technical
documentation to "contain all relevant data or details of the means used by the manufacturer to
ensure that the product with digital elements and the processes put in place by the manufacturer
comply with the essential cybersecurity requirements set out in Annex I". [Annex VII][annex-7],
point 6, lists "reports of the tests carried out to verify the conformity of the product with
digital elements", and [Article 13(22)][article-13] requires the manufacturer to provide, on a
reasoned request from a market surveillance authority, "all the information and documentation, in
paper or electronic form, necessary to demonstrate the conformity of the product with digital
elements". A final result states a conclusion without demonstrating it.

[Article 13(7)][article-13] sets the scale: manufacturers "shall systematically document, in a
manner that is proportionate to the nature and the cybersecurity risks, relevant cybersecurity
aspects concerning the products with digital elements ...". The record can therefore stay small
without losing what makes it checkable:

- Every component carries its final result, its risk level and the reason for that level. For a
  component assessed as low risk, the record can stop there.
- Above low risk, each property checked is one short entry: the property, its outcome, the source
  read and the date. An outcome read from a public source can be looked up again, so the entry is
  its own evidence.
- The bulky material behind an outcome, such as scanner output or test logs, stays out of the
  record. It is kept only where a lookup cannot reproduce it: a property that failed, a property
  the manufacturer judged not applicable, or a test the manufacturer ran. The record names each
  such file by its cryptographic digest, so a file produced years later can be shown to be the one
  the result rested on.

The 10-year floor in [Article 13(13)][article-13] is also a constraint on format. A record held
only as state inside a scanning service depends on that service still existing and still returning
the same answer. A plain, self-describing file that names its own fields can be kept with the
technical documentation. Someone who has never used the tool that wrote it can still read it. Like
the technical documentation, the record is kept at the disposal of the market surveillance
authorities under [Article 13(13)][article-13]; the CRA does not require it to be published or
given to the users of the product.

Where the record is generated, "a commonly used and machine-readable format" is what Annex
I, Part II, point (1) already requires for the software bill of materials. A [JSON
Schema for this record](https://github.com/orcwg/cra-due-diligence/tree/main/schema), with a
worked example and a validator, puts the table above into that form.
