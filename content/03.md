# What and how to record due diligence, evidence

Chapter 1 establishes that [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence is a process the manufacturer
carries out, and that its test is whether the finished product meets the essential cybersecurity
requirements of Annex I. A process leaves no trace unless the manufacturer records it. This
chapter covers what that record contains and who has to be able to read it afterwards.

The CRA does not prescribe a form for the due diligence record and does not place it in a separate
obligation. It seems instead to arrive through the technical documentation. [Article
31][article-31] and Annex VII require the manufacturer to document the assessments carried out,
and [Article 13(13)][article-13] requires manufacturers to "keep the technical documentation and
the EU declaration of conformity at the disposal of the market surveillance authorities for at
least 10 years after the product with digital elements has been placed on the market or for the
support period, whichever is longer". Whoever reads the record was not present when the
manufacturer made the check, and on that retention floor may be reading it a decade afterwards.

## Levels of due diligence

[Recital 34][recital-34] states that the appropriate level of due diligence "depends on the nature
and the level of cybersecurity risk associated with a given component". A component that parses
untrusted input in a privileged context and a component that formats log timestamps do not warrant
the same effort.

The level sets the depth of the checks and the evidence retained for each. It does not govern
whether a record exists at all. A component assessed as low risk still produces a record, and that
record carries the reason the manufacturer set the level where it did. The level is itself a
finding, so its justification belongs alongside the checks it governed. A record that simply omits
the low-risk components is indistinguishable from the record of an assessment that missed them.

## What has to be checked

Chapter 1 sets out three elements, Visibility, Vigilance and Maintenance. Each produces
observations the manufacturer can write down, and the methods listed in [Recital 34][recital-34]
apply across them.

Under Visibility the manufacturer records the component's identity: the name and the exact
revision integrated, its declared dependencies, and where the artefact came from. Annex I,
Part II, point (1) requires the manufacturer in any case to "identify and document vulnerabilities
and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a software
bill of materials in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the
top-level dependencies of the products".

Vigilance is a position at a moment, so it is recorded as one: which databases were consulted on
what date, and what each of them returned, a nil return included. [Recital 34][recital-34]
names the European vulnerability database established pursuant to Article 12(2) of Directive (EU)
2022/2555, and other publicly accessible vulnerability databases.

Maintenance concerns the project's capacity to respond. [Recital 34][recital-34] points to the
security updates history, and a disclosure policy shows the same thing by its presence and its
content. Where an open-source software steward supports the project, [Article 24(1)][article-24]
requires that steward to "put in place and document in a verifiable manner a cybersecurity
policy".

The remaining method in [Recital 34][recital-34], carrying out additional security tests, differs
from the other three in what it leaves behind. The first three record something a third party can
look up and find again. A test records a result that only the party running it observed. A test
result is therefore worth recording with its inputs and the exact revision it ran against, so
that someone else can repeat it.

## What a record has to contain

[Article 13(5)][article-13] attaches due diligence to the act of integrating a component, so the
record attaches to the artefact the manufacturer integrated rather than to the project it came
from. A record naming a project and a version range describes a set of artefacts, so it cannot be
re-checked against the one that shipped.

The CRA does not enumerate fields for this record, so the table that follows is a proposal rather
than a reading of the text. As best we can tell, a record that a later reader can re-perform has
to state at least the following.

| What the record states | Why it is in the record |
| --- | --- |
| The component as integrated, identified by an exact revision and, where the artefact is distributed as a file, its cryptographic digest | Due diligence attaches to the artefact, and a revision plus a digest names one artefact rather than a set |
| The product release the record covers | Due diligence applies to all releases of a product in the product lifecycle |
| What was checked, against which source, and on what date | A later reader consults the same source and sees whether the answer has moved |
| The result, including results that found nothing | A nil return is a check that ran, and an omission is not distinguishable from one that never ran |
| What was done about any finding, which under [Recital 34][recital-34] includes informing the person or entity manufacturing or maintaining the component | The recital names the action, so the record of the action completes the check |
| Who or what performed the check, and the version of any tool used | Two versions of a scanner give two answers about the same component |

The first and third rows separate a record of a check from a record of a conclusion. A conclusion
reports what the manufacturer decided. A check with its inputs supports repeating it, and a market surveillance
authority is in a position to ask what repeating it would yield.

The 10-year floor in [Article 13(13)][article-13] is also a constraint on format. A record held
only as state inside a scanning service depends on that service still existing and still returning
the same answer. A plain, self-describing file that names its own fields travels with the
technical documentation. Someone who has never used the tool that wrote it can still read it.
Where the record is generated, "a commonly used and machine-readable format" is what Annex
I, Part II, point (1) already requires for the software bill of materials.
