# How open source projects and stewards can help manufacturers to achieve due diligence
While the Cyber Resilience Act places 100% of legal product liability on commercial manufacturers, it explicitly recognises the vital role of **Open Source Software Stewards**—legal entities that support open source projects intended for commercial activities.

# Open Source Information & Due Diligence: A Risk-Based Approach Under the CRA

Because the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) mandates a **risk-based approach**, there is no "silver bullet" or one-size-fits-all solution for evaluating open source dependencies. What a manufacturer requires from an open source project depends entirely on the component’s criticality, privileges, and deployment context within the final commercial product.

To satisfy Article 13(5) due diligence obligations effectively without imposing unnecessary friction on maintainers, manufacturers must evaluate upstream projects across four key operational dimensions:

```text
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 RISK-ASSESSMENT EVALUATION DIMENSIONS                       │
├───────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Cybersecurity Policy       │ Documented vulnerability handling & CVD     │
│ 2. Contribution Policy        │ Pathways for contributing security patches  │
│ 3. Project & Community Health │ Long-term maintainer capacity & support     │
│ 4. Licensing & SBOM           │ IP protection embedded in build metadata    │
└───────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

## 1. Cybersecurity Policy & Vulnerability Management

Under the CRA, establishing a documented cybersecurity policy is a core expectation for Open Source Stewards. When conducting a risk assessment, manufacturers must verify how an upstream project handles vulnerability disclosures:

* **Vulnerability Disclosure Channels:** Presence of a clear `SECURITY.md` file, private reporting security advisories, and contact channels.
* **Response & Remediation Workflows:** A documented Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) process indicating predictable response timelines and security release cadences.
* **Impact on Due Diligence:** A robust upstream security policy reassures the manufacturer that discovered flaws will be triaged and addressed systematically rather than ignored.

Having a security policy is only as valuable as its enforcement. Reviewing a project's security history and policy compliance helps manufacturers determine if the component is a safe fit for their product.

## 2. Contribution Policy & Patch Management

Manufacturers are legally obligated under the CRA to provide security updates and patches for active vulnerabilities in their products. If a flaw is discovered in an integrated open source component, the manufacturer must be able to deploy a fix quickly:

* **Upstream Patch Flow:** A defined contribution policy (e.g., `CONTRIBUTING.md`, CLA/DCO requirements) clarifies how a manufacturer's engineering team can contribute security patches directly back to the upstream project.
* **Avoiding Fork Fragmentation:** Understanding the contribution workflow ensures the manufacturer can upstream critical security fixes rather than maintaining indefinite, unmaintained downstream forks.
* **Demonstrating Compliance:** Clear contribution records serve as evidence of proactive vulnerability remediation under Article 13(6) reporting requirements.


## 3. Project & Community Health

A component's security posture is directly tied to the health and sustainability of the community behind it. Manufacturers must assess project health metrics proportional to the component's risk level:

* **Maintainer Activity & Capacity:** Active commit frequency, responsiveness to pull requests, and the "bus factor" (avoiding single-maintainer dependencies for critical components).
* **Long-Term Support:** The project’s track record of maintaining stable branches, handling security releases, and providing clear End-of-Life (EOL) deprecation signals.
* **Capacity to Deliver Fixes:** Verifying that the community has sufficient bandwidth to fulfil its own roadmap and address legally required technical fixes over the product's expected lifespan.


## 4. Licensing Hygiene & SBOM Integration

Legal compliance extends beyond cybersecurity to intellectual property protection. Integrating unverified open source licenses—particularly restrictive copyleft terms—can trigger severe legal and commercial risks:

* **IP Safeguards:** Verifying that all nested component licenses align with commercial deployment models without risking proprietary code exposure.
* **Embedded Metadata via SBOMs:** Licensing metadata should be captured directly within standardized **Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs)** (e.g., SPDX or CycloneDX).
* **Automated Auditing:** Machine-readable SBOM fields allow manufacturers to automatically validate license compliance during CI/CD build pipelines alongside vulnerability scanning.

## Voluntary Security Attestations & The ORCWG Framework

To standardise trust signals without overburdening maintainers, Article 25 empowers the use of **Voluntary Security Attestations**. As developed by the Open Regulatory Compliance Working Group ([orcwg/cra-attestations](https://github.com/orcwg/cra-attestations)), attestations operate along a spectrum rather than a single standard.
* **Light-Weight Attestations:** Low-friction, self-declared signals (e.g., published policies, OpenSSF scorecard results, automated pipeline checks). These satisfy due diligence for low-risk or sandboxed utilities.
* **Elaborated / Certification-Like Attestations:** Comprehensive, high-assurance attestations. These are consumed by manufacturers integrating critical components (e.g., cryptographic modules, kernels, network parsers).

Voluntary Security Attestations hold tangible operational and economic value for commercial manufacturers because they directly offload due diligence tasks that manufacturers would otherwise be legally obligated to perform. Under Article 13(5) of the CRA, commercial entities bear full responsibility for evaluating the security posture, supply chain integrity, and vulnerability management of their open source dependencies. When an open source project or steward produces an attestation, they execute the process checks, pipeline hardening, and metadata generation at the source. By ingesting these harmonised trust signals, manufacturers save significant engineering hours and audit resources that would otherwise be spent conducting redundant, independent security reviews.

Crucially, attestations must not be misconstrued as an obligatory task forced upon open source projects, nor as a "free service" that commercial adopters are entitled to demand. Developing, verifying, and maintaining machine-readable attestations requires dedicated time, technical infrastructure, and organisational overhead. Choosing whether to issue an Article 25 attestation is an autonomous, voluntary decision made entirely by the project or steward.
