# Article 13(5) Due diligence in the scope of the CRA

Due diligence is a general legal term used to describe the duty of care that a party has when making a decision or
undertaking an action. The term dates back to the early modern period and is found in many domains, including European
product liability law. However, the European supranational or "community law" of product security does not offer a
clear, ready to use and general definition of "due diligence". This is largely because due diligence is always
contextual, and the appropriate level of care changes in response to specific facts and circumstances. Legal usage of
the phrase dates back to at least the early modern period and has been part of the language of legal duty ever since.
Perhaps it's this long and varied usage that means there's no easy definition of due diligence under the
Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), as the term can have very different meanings in very different contexts. Legal due diligence
is almost always fact and circumstance based, creating a duty of care for the party performing it. Additionally, within
the context of CRA compliance and at the time of this writing, authoritative sources have yet to offer specific guidance
on due diligence. Informed by available sources, this document provides a consensus of industry and open source experts
on the nature of the due diligence required of manufacturers under the CRA.

Due diligence is used by the CRA to describe the duty that manufacturers have when integrating 3rd party components into
their products, and for the purpose of CRA compliance it must be understood within the context of and from the language
of the Act, supplemented by other authoritative guidance. Due Diligence is first mentioned in
[Recital 21][recital-21] of the CRA and codified as a duty of manufacturers in [Article 13(5)][article-13] as follows:

  > "For the purpose of complying with paragraph 1 (the duty of secure design and development),
    manufacturers shall exercise due diligence when integrating components sourced from third
    parties so that those components do not compromise the cybersecurity of the product with digital
    elements, including when integrating components of free and open-source software that have not
    been made available on the market in the course of a commercial activity"

  > Note: this current version of the whitepaper focuses on due diligence as it is required by
    article 13(5) of the CRA. The ORC WG is in the process of clarifying the continuity and scope of
    due diligence in issue [#5](https://github.com/orcwg/cra-due-diligence/issues/5) and
    [#14](https://github.com/orcwg/cra-due-diligence/issues/14). 

Due diligence under [Article 13(5)][article-13] is the duty of manufacturers to ensure that the components of their
products do not compromise the product's security within the meaning of the CRA's Essential Cybersecurity Requirements
provided by [Annex I, Part I][annex-1]. Beyond these requirements, manufacturers also have additional duties of
care related to component integration, including those related to non-remote data processing solution services
(see July 2026's Guidance from the European Commission, 8.1.3 [199 - 201][july-2026-ec-guideance-p199]), and the
requirements of CRA [Article 13(6)][article-13], [Article 13(8)][article-13], and [Annex II, Part II][annex-2].
These additional requirements are out of scope of present whitepaper.

[Article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence is the process by which manufacturers can demonstrate that
the integration of a new component does not compromise the security posture of the final product, a
demonstration that is becoming increasingly critical under the CRA. The details of exactly how to
accomplish this are less clear, though ultimately they come down to the manufacturer knowing the
details of the components their product contains, making sure they are fit for the product's
security requirements, and allowing the manufacturer to take action to mitigate any known exploitable
vulnerabilities.

## Overview of Due Diligence Process

The details of exactly how a manufacturer should perform due diligence are less clear, though they ultimately require
that a manufacturer know the details of the components their product contains, ensure that the component is fit for the
product's security requirements, and take actions to mitigate any known exploitable vulnerabilities within those
components. 

While the Act’s uses the term due diligence in [article 13(5)][article-13] to discuss risk assessment requirements, the
proper integration of 3rd party components appears to demand more: that a manufacturer implement a risk assessment and
risk management process aligned with the Act’s focus on security throughout the product lifecycle and consider the
following elements:

* **Visibility**: The manufacturer's ability to analyse components and make a valid assessment of thier risks and
  security features. The availability of a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) is typically instrumental in achieving this
  objective, as the CRA emphasises the need for transparency and documented evidence regarding the security properties
  of product components. However, since the CRA does not require public disclosure of SBOMs
  (see [Recital 77][recital-77]) it may be difficult for integrators to obtain SBOMs for components, and other
  activities including testing, attestations, or contractual requirements may be needed.
* **Vigilance**: The CRA mandates continuous compliance, making the ability to monitor for existing vulnerabilities
  essential for understanding the level of exposure a manufacturer assumes upon integration of a component. This ongoing
  vigilance is a core requirement for maintaining a secure product over its expected lifetime.
* **Maintenance**: The level of community activity is an important factor for determining the risk profile that a
  particular component introduces to a product. A poorly maintained component represents a higher risk of unpatched
  vulnerabilities, which directly conflicts with the CRA's requirement for manufacturers to ensure the timely and
  effective handling of security updates. CRA requires the due diligence to be done at the integation time of 3rd party
  components, including open source components to the products, and throughout the product's lifecycle. Due diligence
  should be performed for all releases of a product in the product lifecycle.

Performing due diligence is an obligation to manage the risks of integrating a third-party component, and for free and
open source software (FOSS) this will more frequently include manufacturer responsibility to assess the open source
component's risks directly.
The CRA requires the [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence to be done at the integration time of 3rd party
components, including open source components to the products, and throughout the product's lifecycle. Due diligence
should be done for all releases of a product in the product lifecycle. The application of the principle of
[article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence with respect to open source should be understood as an obligation to manage
the risk of using any third-party component plus the responsibility to assess the open source component's risks
yourself. Open source constitutes a vast and diverse ecosystem, ranging from massive, professionally supported projects
to small, volunteer-maintained repositories. This inherent diversity, in terms of community activity, maintenance
practices, and documentation quality, presents a fundamental challenge to consistently and accurately assessing the
three key [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence elements (Visibility, Vigilance, and Maintenance) required under the
CRA.

Even if the specific requirements of [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence under the CRA have yet to be offered by
the European Commission or courts, it is possible to make several educated guesses about how it will work.

1. CRA [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence applies to 3rd party components of a product, including components that
   are also part of a Remote Data Processing Solution.
1. The primary purpose and test of successful due diligence is that the completed product meets [Annex I's][annex-1]
   essential cybersecurity requirements and that the manufacturer performs elements of vulnerability handling impacted
   by components and the product supply chain, such as the creation of an SBOM.

   > Note: This 2nd point depends on the interpretation of the scope of due diligence discussed in
     issue [#14](https://github.com/orcwg/cra-due-diligence/issues/14) and will be updated once the issue is resolved.

In the future, the European Commission may establish specific requirements on [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence,
including or as part of a voluntary security attestation program established via a delegated act as provided for in
[Article 25][article-25].
Currently, however, specifics remain elusive.

Beyond these basic duties, the CRA and related Commission publications have offered additional clarifications regarding
[article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence. The most significant of these is found in [Recital 34][recital-34]
of the CRA where the Act notes several potential methods of performing the non-attestation parts of
[article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence on 3rd party components:

1. Verifying that the component demonstrates conformity with the CRA, such as by using CE marked
   components. 
1. Verifying that the component regularly receives security updates.
1. Verifying that the component is free from vulnerabilities registered in the European shared
   vulnerability database created by the NIS2 Directive (EU Directive 2022/2555) or other publicly 
   available vulnerability databases.
1. Performing security tests on the component.

[July 2026's Guidance from the European Commission][july-2026-ec-guideance] section 8.2 (207 - 208) also includes
additional potential means of performing due diligence, specifically within the context of remote data processing
solution services: 

1. Verifying that the component demonstrates conformity with other appropriate security regulations and directives such
   as NIS 2, via [(EU) 2024/2690][2024-2690] or DORA ([(EU) 2022/2554][2022-2554]).
1. Verifying that the component demonstrates conformity with appropriate ISO standards
   ([ISO/IEC 27017:2015][ISO-27017-2015] or [ISO/IEC 27001:2022][ISO-27001-2022]) or EUCC certification program.


## Properties to check for Article 13(5) due diligence

The purpose of [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence is to ensure that the integrated open source components are in
a state that they do not compromise the cybersecurity of the product. 
This can mean that the development practices ensuring good code quality, have a way to report
vulnerabilities, react to the reported vulnerabilities and release fixes.
This chapter collects a selection of example properties what can be checked as the [article 13(5)][article-13] due
diligence.
The CRA introduces the concept of a voluntary security attestation as a way for open source communities to support the
manufacturer's due diligence obligations. Such a voluntary attestation could provide the necessary data and assurances
about the project properties identified in this chapter to carry out [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence.
Currently, the concept is still under development, therefore requiring manufacturers to assess individual properties
and characteristics of open source projects, as exemplified in the following sections.

### Project identity and authenticity

Open source projects are forked in many cases. One primary reason is the contribution workflow of
major software development platforms where the open source projects are developed (e.g.: github.com
and gitlab.com). Another reason can be that a developer or a set of developers would like to
drive the project to a different direction, would like to experiment with the technology or do not agree with the
governance or license changes of the project. When integrating open source components, the source of these components
should almost always be the major upstream, and should never be an unmaintained fork. If a fork is used, it should be
well-maintained (adopting security fixes from upstream, at a minimum) and adopted for a particular justified reason.
If the project has an open source software steward
the steward could be a good entity of clarify the identity of the project in case of a doubt.

### Development practices

Development practices of the integrated components are a critial part of [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence. The
correct development practices ensure that the code of the component is always peer reviewed and tested.

**Number of approvals for reviews:** The project should conduct peer reviews for every change. This
means that all changes should have a review process (GitHub Pull Request, GitLab Merge Request, Gerrit
Change Set or similar) and there should ideally be at least one approval from a person who is not the
proposer of the change. Peer reviews allow to identify and prevent attempts to inject malicious code into a project.

**Automatic software testing:** The project should have automatic testing set up which runs for all
change requests before the changes are merged. This aims to maintain software quality, voiding regressions, and lowering
the risk of introducing vulnerabilities.

**Regular releases:** The project should have regular releases. In this way the vulnerability fixes of
the project are consumable by the products integrating the project. In case of stable projects without vulnerabilities
to be addressed regular releases might not be needed. 

### Governance

**Sustainable governance:** The project is governed in a way that is sustainable for the foreseeable duration of the
product life-cycle and minimum support period. Examples may include having a published project succession plan, an
active core group of co-maintainers, or a registered non-profit legal entity governing the project.

### Vulnerability handling

Naturally it is a critical factor of [article 13(5)][article-13] due diligence how the projects handle vulnerabilities.

**SECURITY.md:** The project should have a `SECURITY.md` or similar file which describes the security
and vulnerability handling policy of the project, including for instance how users can report vulnerabilities
and which branches and releases are supported (i.e., receive fixes).

**Vulnerability handling:** On a long-term perspective, the project should not neglect vulnerabilities. The reported
vulnerabilities should be addressed in a timely manner.
