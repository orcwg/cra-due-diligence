# 1. Introduction

Due diligence is a general legal term used to describe the duty of care that a party has when making a decision or undertaking an action. The term dates back to the early modern period and is found in many domains, including European product liability law.  However, the European supranational or “community law” of product liability doesn’t offer a clear, ready to use and general definition of "due diligence". This is largely because due diligence is always contextual, and the appropriate level of care changes in response to specific facts and circumstances. Additionally, within the context of Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) compliance and at the time of this writing, authoritative sources have yet to offer specific guidance on due diligence. Informed by available sources, this document provides a consensus of industry and open source experts on the nature of the due diligence required of manufacturers under the CRA.

## 1.1 Due Diligence Within the Scope of the CRA
Due diligence is used by the CRA to describe the duty that manufacturers have when integrating 3rd party components into their products, and for the purpose of CRA compliance it must be understood within the context of and from the language of the Act, supplemented by other authoritative guidance. Due diligence is first mentioned in 
[Recital 21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#rct_21) of the CRA and codified as a duty of manufacturers in [Article 13(5)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#art_13) as follows:

"For the purpose of complying with paragraph 1 [the duty of secure design and development], manufacturers shall exercise due diligence when integrating components sourced from third parties so that those components do not compromise the cybersecurity of the product with digital elements, including when integrating components of free and open-source software that have not been made available on the market in the course of a commcercial activity.” 

Due diligence under [Article 13(5)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#art_13) is the duty of manufacturers to ensure that the components of their products do not compromise the product's security within the meaning of the CRA's Essential Cybersecurity Requirements provided by [Annex I, Part I](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#anx_I). Beyond these requirements, manufacturers also have additional duties of care related to component integration, including those related to non-remote data processing solution services (see Draft Guidance, 8.1.3 [183 - 185]), and the requirements of CRA [Article 13(6)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#art_13), [Article 13(8)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#art_13), and [Annex II, Part II](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#anx_II).

## 1.2 Overview of Due Diligence Process
The details of exactly how a manufacturer should perform due diligence are less clear, though they ultimately require that a manufacturer know the details of the components their product contains, ensure that the component is fit for the product's security requirements, and take actions to mitigate any known exploitable vulnerabilities within those components. 

While the Act’s uses the term due diligence in Article 13 (5) to discuss risk assessment requirements, the proper integration of 3rd party components appears to demand more: that a manufacturer implement a risk assessment and risk management process aligned with the Act’s focus on security throughout the product lifecycle and consider the following elements:

* **Visibility**: The manufacturer's ability to analyse components and make a valid assessment of thier risks and security features. The availability of a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) is typically instrumental in achieving this objective, as the CRA emphasises the need for transparency and documented evidence regarding the security properties of product components. However, since the CRA does not require public disclosure of SBOMs (see [Recital 77](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#rct_77)) it may be difficult for integrators to obtain SBOMs for components, and other activities including testing, attestations, or contractual requirements may be needed. 
* **Vigilance**: The CRA mandates continuous compliance, making the ability to monitor for existing vulnerabilities essential for understanding the level of exposure a manufacturer assumes upon integration of a component. This ongoing vigilance is a core requirement for maintaining a secure product over its expected lifetime.
* **Maintenance**: The level of community activity is an important factor for determining the risk profile that a particular component introduces to a product. A poorly maintained component represents a higher risk of unpatched vulnerabilities, which directly conflicts with the CRA's requirement for manufacturers to ensure the timely and effective handling of security updates. CRA requires the due diligence to be done at the integation time of 3rd party components, including open source components to the products, and throughout the product's lifecycle. Due diligence should be performed for all releases of a product in the product lifecycle.

Performing due diligence is an obligation to manage the risks of integrating a third-party component, and for free and open source software (FOSS) this will more frequently include manufacturer responsibility to assess the open source component's risks directly. FOSS is a vast and diverse ecosystem, ranging from massive, professionally supported projects to small, volunteer-maintained repositories. This inherent diversity, in terms of community activity, maintenance practices, and documentation quality, presents a fundamental challenge to consistently and accurately assessing the three key due
diligence elements (Visibility, Vigilance, and Maintenance) required under the CRA.

Even if the specific requirements of component integration due diligence under the CRA have yet to be offered by the European Commission or courts, it is possibile to make several educated guesses about how it will work.

1. CRA due diligence applies to 3rd party components of a product, including remote services that perform product functions but may not be Remoe Data Processing Solutions.   
2. The primary purpose and test of successful due diligence is that the completed product meets
  Annex I's essential cybersecurity requirements and that the manufacturer performs elements of
  vulnerability handling impacted by components and the product supply chain, such as the creation
  of an SBOM.

In the future, the European Commission may establish specific requirements on due diligence, including or as part of a voluntary security attestation program established via delegated act as provided for in [Article 25](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#art_25). Currently specifics remain illusive.

Beyond these basic duties, the CRA and related Commission publications have offered additional clarifications regarding due diligence. The most significant of these is found in [Recital 34](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847#rct_34) of the CRA where the Act notes several potential methods of performing the non-attestation parts of due
diligence on 3rd party components:

1. Verifying that the component demonstrates conformity with the CRA, such as by using CE marked
   components.
2. Verifying that the component regularly receives security updates.
3. Verifying that the component is free from vulnerabilities registered in the European shared vulnerability database created by the NIS2 Directive (EU Directive 2022/2555) or other publically available vulnerability databases.
4. Performing security tests on the component.

March 2026's Draft Guidance from the European Commission section 8.2 (191 - 192) also includes additional potential means of performing due diligence, specifically within the context of non-remote data processing solution services: 

1. Verifying that the component demonstrates conformity with other appropriate security regulations and directives such as NIS 2 or DORA.
2. Verifying that the component demonstrates conformity with appropriate ISO standards or EUCC certification program.
3. Negotiating contractal assurances that 3rd party providers will perform vulnerability handling and other promised security functions, and share information regarding updates and other changes to the component or service.
